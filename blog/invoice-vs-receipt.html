Invoice vs receipt: what's the difference?

One asks for money. The other proves it arrived. Sending the wrong one creates real accounting problems.

An invoice is a request for payment issued before money changes hands. A receipt is proof of payment issued after. They look similar, they are not interchangeable, and mixing them up is one of the most common small-business paperwork errors.

When a freelancer sends an invoice

Every time you want to be paid: after delivering work, on a milestone, monthly for a retainer, or up front for a deposit. The invoice states what was delivered, how much is owed, and by when. Full field list in what to include on an invoice.

When a freelancer sends a receipt

When the client asks for one — and increasingly often they will, because their bookkeeping requires it. A receipt is usually requested when:

  • the client paid by bank transfer and needs proof for their accounts;
  • the client paid personally and is claiming the cost back as an expense;
  • the client is registered for VAT/GST and needs the document for a reclaim;
  • the payment was made in cash, where no processor generates proof;
  • a deposit was taken and the client wants confirmation before work starts.

If your client pays by card or through a payment link, the processor usually emails a receipt automatically — you may not need to issue anything. If they pay by transfer, a one-page confirmation takes two minutes and prevents a month of back-and-forth.

Can one document be both?

Yes, and for small transactions it often should be. You can issue an invoice marked “PAID” with the payment date and method added, or a combined document titled “Invoice / Receipt”. What matters is that the document is unambiguous: if it says paid, it must state the date and method, and the amount must match the money that actually arrived. An invoice marked paid that was only partially paid is worse than no receipt at all.

What a receipt must contain

  • The word “Receipt” (or “Paid”) clearly at the top.
  • A receipt number, ideally from the same sequence family as your invoices.
  • Date of payment, and the amount received in the invoiced currency.
  • Payment method and, if relevant, a transaction reference.
  • What the payment was for — the invoice number it settles, plus a short description.
  • Your business name and tax number if you are registered.

Other documents people confuse with these two

  • Quote / estimate — a proposed price before work starts. Not a request for payment.
  • Pro forma invoice — a preview of the final invoice, often used to clear customs or start a purchase-order process. Not a demand for payment and not a receipt.
  • Purchase order — the client's document authorising the spend. Your invoice usually has to quote its number.
  • Credit note — a negative invoice, issued when you refund or reduce a charge.
  • Statement of account — a summary of several invoices and what is still outstanding.

How this affects getting paid

Invoices get paid faster when they are complete and unambiguous, because the client's accounts payable team has nothing to query. A vague line item, a missing purchase-order number or the wrong client entity will hold up an otherwise correct invoice — and none of those are fixed by sending a receipt.
