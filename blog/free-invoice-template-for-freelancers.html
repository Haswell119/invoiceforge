Free invoice template for freelancers: what to put on it

The fields that get invoices paid, the ones people forget, and how to skip the template download entirely.

Most freelancer invoices are missing at least two things a client's bookkeeper needs to pay without emailing you. This is a short, practical checklist of what belongs on an invoice — and an honest look at whether you need to download a template file at all.

The eight things that actually matter

  1. Your legal name and address — whatever entity is receiving the money, plus the address on the bank account. A trading name alone causes questions at payment time.
  2. A unique invoice number — sequential and never reused. How to choose a numbering scheme.
  3. Issue date and a specific due date — "Due 14 March", not "Net 30". A real date is the only thing a bookkeeper can put in a payment run without interpreting it.
  4. The client's billing entity — the exact company that will pay, not the brand name on their website. This is the single most common cause of a "who is this invoice from?" delay.
  5. Line items with quantity and rate — descriptions in the client's own words where possible ("March landing page", "workshop, 18 March"). Disputes happen at line-item level; a line item that can be matched to something doesn't get argued with.
  6. Subtotal, tax and total — shown separately, with the tax rate visible. If you are not registered for VAT/GST, say so explicitly on the document rather than leaving the field blank.
  7. Payment details and payment terms — bank details, currency, and any late-payment terms. Terms written on the invoice are read; terms that live only in your contract are not.
  8. A reference your client recognises — their PO number, project code, or the person who commissioned it. If a client asks for a PO number, put it on the invoice.

Do you need to download a template at all?

A .docx or .xlsx template works, and for years it was the only free option. Two things tend to happen to it: the file drifts (someone edits the footer, the totals formula breaks) and it lives on whichever machine you last used. Neither is fatal, but both add friction to a task you do every month.
